Lotus Proof

Proves that expected values of transformed random variables can be computed directly from the original distribution, avoiding the need to derive the transformed variable's distribution

💬 Discussion (2 messages)
Where do you get a variable's distribution from in the first place?

Great question! This gets at something fundamental that the LOTUS proof takes as a given. A random variable's distribution comes from modeling the underlying random phenomenon. There are typically three main sources:

1. Theoretical/Physical Reasoning: We derive the distribution from the nature of the process. For example, if you're counting successes in $n$ independent trials with probability $p$, that's a Binomial distribution. If you're measuring time between random events (like customer arrivals), that's often modeled as Exponential with some rate $\lambda$. The document's example uses $f(x) = \lambda e^{-\lambda x}$ for $x \geq 0$—this exponential PDF models "waiting times" in many real-world scenarios.

2. Empirical Data: You collect data and fit a distribution to it. You might observe that your data looks bell-shaped and estimate parameters $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$ for a Normal distribution, or use statistical tests to determine which theoretical distribution best matches your observations.

3. Given as Part of the Problem: In textbooks and applications, you're often simply told "let $X \sim \text{Exponential}(\lambda)$" or "suppose $X$ has PDF $f(x) = 2x$ for $x \in [0,1]$." The distribution is your starting point.

The beauty of LOTUS is that once you have $f_X(x)$ or $p_X(x)$ from any of these sources, you can compute $E[g(X)]$ directly without having to derive the (often complicated) distribution of $Y = g(X)$ first!

Introduction

LOTUS allows us to compute E[g(X)] directly from the distribution of X, without needing to find the distribution of g(X) first. This is one of the most practically useful results in probability theory.

Statement

★ LOTUS (Discrete)
$$E[g(X)] = \sum_x g(x) \cdot p(x)$$
For discrete X with PMF p(x)
★ LOTUS (Continuous)
$$E[g(X)] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} g(x) \cdot f(x) \, dx$$
For continuous X with PDF f(x)

Proof (Discrete Case)

Let Y = g(X). We start from the definition of expectation and show it reduces to summing over x instead of y.

→ Step 1: Definition
$$E[Y] = \sum_y y \cdot P(Y = y)$$
Begin with the standard definition of expected value
→ Step 2: Probability decomposition
$$P(Y = y) = \sum_{x:\, g(x) = y} p(x)$$
Sum probabilities of all x values mapping to y
→ Step 3: Substitute
$$E[Y] = \sum_y y \cdot \sum_{x:\, g(x) = y} p(x)$$
Replace P(Y=y) with the sum over preimages
→ Step 4: Rearrange
$$= \sum_y \sum_{x:\, g(x) = y} y \cdot p(x)$$
Move y inside the inner sum
→ Step 5: Substitute g(x)
$$= \sum_y \sum_{x:\, g(x) = y} g(x) \cdot p(x)$$
Since y = g(x) for each term in the inner sum
∴ Result (Discrete)
$$= \sum_x g(x) \cdot p(x) \quad \blacksquare$$
The double sum covers each x exactly once since each x maps to exactly one y

Proof (Continuous Case)

Let Y = g(X). We assume g is monotonic and differentiable for simplicity. The proof uses the change of variables technique.

For Strictly Increasing g

→ Step 1: CDF of Y
$$F_Y(y) = P(g(X) \leq y) = P(X \leq g^{-1}(y)) = F_X(g^{-1}(y))$$
Transform the event using the inverse function
→ Step 2: PDF of Y
$$f_Y(y) = f_X(g^{-1}(y)) \cdot \frac{d}{dy}g^{-1}(y)$$
Differentiate the CDF using the chain rule
→ Step 3: Definition
$$E[Y] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} y \cdot f_Y(y) \, dy$$
Start with the definition of expectation for Y
→ Step 4: Substitution
$$u = g^{-1}(y), \quad y = g(u), \quad dy = g'(u)\, du$$
Set up the change of variables
→ Step 5: Transform integral
$$E[Y] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} g(u) \cdot f_X(u) \cdot \frac{1}{g'(u)} \cdot g'(u) \, du$$
Apply substitution—note the g'(u) terms cancel
→ Step 6: Simplify
$$= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} g(u) \cdot f_X(u) \, du$$
The derivative terms cancel completely
∴ Result (Continuous)
$$= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} g(x) \cdot f_X(x) \, dx \quad \blacksquare$$
Rename dummy variable u back to x

Why "Unconscious"?

The name comes from the fact that students often use this formula without realizing it needs proof—they "unconsciously" assume you can just substitute g(x) into the expectation formula. The theorem validates this intuition!

Example Application

Find E[X²] where X ~ Exponential(λ). Using LOTUS, we can compute this directly without finding the distribution of X² first.

= Direct Calculation
$$E[X^2] = \int_0^{\infty} x^2 \cdot \lambda e^{-\lambda x} \, dx = \frac{2}{\lambda^2}$$
LOTUS lets us integrate against the original exponential PDF
✎ Key Advantage
$$\text{LOTUS: integrate } g(x) f_X(x) \quad \text{vs} \quad \text{derive } f_{X^2} \text{ then integrate}$$
Without LOTUS, you'd need to find the distribution of X² first—much harder!
