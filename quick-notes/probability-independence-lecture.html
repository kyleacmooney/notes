Probability Independence Lecture

1. Review of Conditional Probability

We start with a sample space Ω representing an experiment. When told that event B has occurred, outcomes outside B are no longer possible. Effectively, B becomes our new sample space, and we must reassign probabilities to remaining outcomes such that they sum to 1 within this new universe.

:= Conditional Probability
$$P(A|B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}$$
The probability of A given B occurred, assuming P(B) > 0

Intuition: We look at the probability of the intersection A ∩ B (the part of A inside B) and normalize by the total probability of B. It represents the fraction of B's probability mass that is also assigned to A.

1.1. Key Tools from Conditional Probability

★ Multiplication Rule
$$P(A \cap B) = P(B) \cdot P(A|B)$$
Calculate joint probability by chaining: first B happens, then A given B
★ Total Probability Theorem
$$P(B) = P(A)P(B|A) + P(A^c)P(B|A^c)$$
Divide and conquer: partition the sample space into scenarios and sum
✎ General Partition
$$P(B) = \sum_i P(A_i)P(B|A_i)$$
Extends to any partition A₁, A₂, ..., Aₙ of the sample space
★ Bayes' Rule
$$P(A_i|B) = \frac{P(A_i)P(B|A_i)}{P(B)}$$
Reverse conditional probabilities: infer cause from observed effect
= Bayes' Rule (Expanded)
$$P(A_i|B) = \frac{P(A_i)P(B|A_i)}{\sum_j P(A_j)P(B|A_j)}$$
Using Total Probability Theorem for the denominator

2. Models and Inference

In real-world applications, we model systems using a tree structure: Stage 1 (Nature/Cause) where the environment selects a state, and Stage 2 (Measurement/Effect) where a device produces a reading. Inference is observing the measurement and calculating the probability of being on a specific branch.

2.1. Example: Three Coin Tosses

Consider three consecutive tosses of a biased coin where P(Heads) = p. The probability tree splits at each node into H (prob p) and T (prob 1-p). The crucial observation: the conditional probability P(H₂|...) is always p regardless of history—this is independence.

→ Multiplication Rule Application
$$P(T \cap H \cap T) = (1-p) \cdot p \cdot (1-p)$$
Multiply probabilities along the specific path T-H-T in the tree
→ Total Probability Application
$$P(\text{1H}) = p(1-p)^2 + (1-p)p(1-p) + (1-p)^2 p = 3p(1-p)^2$$
Sum over all disjoint paths with exactly one head: HTT, THT, TTH

2.2. Bayes' Rule Application

= Setup
$$P(H_1 | \text{1H}) = \frac{P(H_1 \cap \text{1H})}{P(\text{1H})}$$
Given exactly 1 head, find probability first toss was heads
→ Numerator
$$P(H_1 \cap \text{1H}) = P(H,T,T) = p(1-p)^2$$
Only sequence HTT satisfies both conditions
∴ Result
$$P(H_1 | \text{1H}) = \frac{p(1-p)^2}{3p(1-p)^2} = \frac{1}{3}$$
By symmetry, one head among three slots is equally likely in any position

3. Independence of Two Events

Two events are independent if the occurrence of one provides no information about the occurrence of the other. Our beliefs about B do not change upon learning A.

:= Intuitive Definition
$$P(B|A) = P(B)$$
Learning A occurred doesn't change our probability assessment of B
:= Formal Definition
$$P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B)$$
Symmetric form that handles P(A)=0 case; the standard mathematical definition

3.1. Independence vs. Disjointness

✎ Common Confusion
$$A \cap B = \emptyset \implies P(B|A) = 0$$
Disjoint events are extremely DEPENDENT, not independent

If A and B are disjoint, then if A occurs, B cannot occur. Unless P(B) was already 0, knowing A occurred drastically changed the probability of B. Information about A gives perfect information about B (that it didn't happen).

4. Conditional Independence

:= Conditional Independence
$$P(A \cap B | C) = P(A | C) \cdot P(B | C)$$
Within the universe C, learning A provides no additional information about B

4.1. Independence Does NOT Imply Conditional Independence

Example: Two independent coin flips. Let A = 1st toss is Heads, B = 2nd toss is Heads, C = two tosses had the same result (HH or TT). Unconditionally, A and B are independent.

→ Under Conditioning
$$P(B | A \cap C) = 1 \neq \frac{1}{2} = P(B | C)$$
Knowing both C (same result) and A (first is H) forces B to be H

4.2. Conditional Independence Does NOT Imply Unconditional Independence

Example: Two unfair coins (Coin A: P(H)=0.9, Coin B: P(H)=0.1). Pick one at random and flip twice. Given which coin we picked, the two tosses are independent.

→ Conditional Independence
$$P(H_2 | H_1, \text{Coin A}) = P(H_2 | \text{Coin A}) = 0.9$$
Given the coin, tosses are independent
✎ Unconditional Dependence
$$P(H_2 | H_1) \neq P(H_2)$$
Without knowing the coin, seeing H₁ suggests we likely have the biased-toward-heads coin
