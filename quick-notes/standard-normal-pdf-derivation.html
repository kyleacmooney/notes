Standard Normal Pdf Derivation

Derives the normalizing constant for the Gaussian distribution using the polar coordinate trick, showing why the factor 1/√(2π) appears in the standard normal PDF.

1. Starting Point: The Form of the PDF

The normal distribution arises naturally from Gauss's study of measurement errors. If errors are symmetric, independent, and the most likely value is the mean, the distribution must have exponential-quadratic form.

⊢ Gaussian Form
$$f(x) = c \cdot e^{-ax^2}$$
The general form forced by symmetry and independence assumptions.
▶ ✦ Why Errors Follow the Gaussian

Measurement errors are the small, unavoidable discrepancies between a measured value and the "true" value. When you measure something repeatedly—say, the length of a table—you'll get slightly different results each time due to instrument limitations, environmental fluctuations, or human factors. These deviations from the true value are measurement errors.

Why the Gaussian emerges: Gauss showed that if we assume three reasonable properties about errors, the Gaussian is the only distribution that satisfies all of them:

1. Symmetry: Positive and negative errors of equal magnitude are equally likely. If you're off by $+0.1$, that should be just as probable as being off by $-0.1$. Mathematically, this means $f(x) = f(-x)$, so the distribution must be an even function—depending only on $x^2$.

2. Independence: Errors in different measurements don't affect each other. For independent random variables, their joint probability is the product: $f(x)f(y) = g(x^2 + y^2)$ for some function $g$. This is a functional equation, and the only continuous solution where $f$ is even is the exponential form $f(x) = c \cdot e^{-ax^2}$.

3. Most likely value = mean: The maximum of the error distribution occurs at zero (no error). Taking $\frac{d}{dx}[c \cdot e^{-ax^2}] = 0$ confirms the peak is at $x=0$, and we need $a > 0$ for this to be a maximum rather than minimum.

These three constraints uniquely determine the Gaussian form $f(x) = c \cdot e^{-ax^2}$. No other distribution satisfies all three simultaneously—this is why the Gaussian is so fundamental in statistics and physics.

= Standard Normal (a = 1/2)
$$f(x) = c \cdot e^{-x^2/2}$$
Setting mean = 0 and variance = 1 forces a = 1/2.

The question becomes: what is the normalizing constant c?

2. The Normalization Requirement

⊢ PDF Condition
$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\, dx = 1$$
Every PDF must integrate to 1 over its support.
= Normalization Equation
$$c \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2/2}\, dx = 1$$
Substituting our proposed form.
= Formula for c
$$c = \frac{1}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2/2}\, dx}$$
We need to evaluate this integral.

The problem: the integral of e^(-x²/2) has no elementary antiderivative. Standard techniques like u-substitution and integration by parts fail.

3. The Gaussian Integral: A Clever Trick

:= Define the Integral
$$I = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2/2}\, dx$$
Give the unknown integral a name.

The key insight (attributed to Poisson, building on Laplace): instead of computing I directly, compute I².

Square the Integral

→ Step 1: Write I²
$$I^2 = \left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2/2}\, dx\right) \cdot \left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-y^2/2}\, dy\right)$$
Use different dummy variables (x and y) for each integral.
→ Step 2: Combine Integrands
$$I^2 = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2/2} \cdot e^{-y^2/2}\, dx\, dy$$
Independent integrals combine into a double integral.
→ Step 3: Simplify Exponent
$$I^2 = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-(x^2 + y^2)/2}\, dx\, dy$$
The exponent x² + y² = r² is radial distance—this suggests polar coordinates!

Convert to Polar Coordinates

The function e^(-(x²+y²)/2) is rotationally symmetric—it depends only on distance from the origin. Polar coordinates will exploit this symmetry.

= Polar Substitution
$$x = r\cos\theta, \quad y = r\sin\theta, \quad dx\,dy = r\,dr\,d\theta$$
The Jacobian contributes a factor of r.
→ Step 4: Transform Region
$$I^2 = \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^{\infty} e^{-r^2/2} \cdot r\, dr\, d\theta$$
The entire xy-plane maps to r ∈ [0,∞) and θ ∈ [0,2π).
→ Step 5: Separate Integrals
$$I^2 = \left(\int_0^{2\pi} d\theta\right) \cdot \left(\int_0^{\infty} r \cdot e^{-r^2/2}\, dr\right)$$
The integrand factors; limits don't mix.
= Angular Integral
$$\int_0^{2\pi} d\theta = 2\pi$$
Full rotation around the origin.

The Radial Integral

This is where the magic happens. The factor r from the Jacobian makes this integral elementary!

→ Step 6: u-Substitution
$$u = \frac{r^2}{2} \implies du = r\,dr$$
The r·dr term matches du exactly.
→ Step 7: Evaluate
$$\int_0^{\infty} r \cdot e^{-r^2/2}\, dr = \int_0^{\infty} e^{-u}\, du = \left[-e^{-u}\right]_0^{\infty} = 1$$
Standard exponential integral.

Combine Results

→ Step 8: Compute I²
$$I^2 = 2\pi \cdot 1 = 2\pi$$
Product of angular and radial integrals.
∴ Gaussian Integral
$$I = \sqrt{2\pi}$$
Take positive root since I is a positive integral.

4. The Final Answer

= Normalizing Constant
$$c = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}$$
From our formula c = 1/I.
∴ Standard Normal PDF
$$\boxed{f(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-x^2/2}}$$
The complete probability density function.

5. Verification

→ Check Normalization
$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-x^2/2}\, dx = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \cdot \sqrt{2\pi} = 1 \quad \checkmark$$
Confirms the PDF integrates to 1 as required.

Key Insights

✎ Why Polar Works
$$e^{-(x^2+y^2)/2} = e^{-r^2/2}$$
Rotational symmetry means the function depends only on distance from origin.
✎ The Essential Jacobian
$$dx\,dy = r\,dr\,d\theta$$
Without the factor r, the radial integral would remain intractable.
✎ Geometric Origin of √(2π)
$$I = \sqrt{I^2} = \sqrt{2\pi \cdot 1}$$
The 2π comes from integrating around a full circle; square root from squaring I.
