Lecture 2 Conditioning And Bayes Rule

1. Review: Countable Additivity and Continuous Models

⊢ Countable Additivity
$$P(A_1 \cup A_2 \cup \cdots) = P(A_1) + P(A_2) + \cdots$$
Applies only to countable (sequence) unions of disjoint events.
¶ The Point Paradox

Consider a unit square as sample space with P(A) = area(A). The square is the union of all its points, each point has area zero, so shouldn't P(□) = 0? But we know P(□) = 1. The resolution: additivity applies only to countable unions, and the points in a square form an uncountable set.

✎ Zero Probability ≠ Impossible
$$P(A) = 0 \;\not\Rightarrow\; A \text{ is impossible}, \quad P(A) = 1 \;\not\Rightarrow\; A \text{ is certain}$$
In continuous models, every specific outcome has probability zero, yet outcomes do occur.

2. Conditional Probability

¶ Motivation

You have prior beliefs about outcomes. Then you learn that event B occurred. How should you update? Once told B occurred, B becomes your new universe: outcomes outside B are impossible, and probabilities within B must be rescaled to sum to 1 while preserving relative proportions.

:= Conditional Probability
$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}, \quad \text{for } P(B) > 0$$
Out of the total probability assigned to B, what fraction belongs to outcomes where A also occurs?
★ Conditional Probability Is a Valid Probability Law
$$P(\cdot \mid B) \text{ satisfies: (1) Non-negativity, (2) } P(B \mid B) = 1, \text{ (3) Additivity}$$
All probability theorems apply equally to conditional probabilities.
✎ Uniform Distribution Shortcut
$$\text{If } P \text{ is uniform on } \Omega, \text{ then } P(\cdot \mid B) \text{ is uniform on } B \text{ with } P(\omega \mid B) = \frac{1}{|B|}$$
Conditioning on B preserves uniform distribution within B.

3. The Multiplication Rule

★ Multiplication Rule (Two Events)
$$P(A \cap B) = P(B) \cdot P(A \mid B)$$
Probability both occur = P(B happens) × P(A happens given B already did).
★ Chain Rule (Three Events)
$$P(A \cap B \cap C) = P(A) \cdot P(B \mid A) \cdot P(C \mid A \cap B)$$
Generalizes to any finite number of events; multiply conditional probabilities along tree branches.

4. The Total Probability Theorem

¶ Divide-and-Conquer Strategy

Partition the sample space into disjoint scenarios A₁, A₂, A₃, ... To find P(B), calculate P(B) under each scenario and take a weighted average by how likely each scenario is.

★ Total Probability Theorem
$$P(B) = \sum_i P(A_i) \cdot P(B \mid A_i)$$
Weighted average of P(B) across all scenarios, weighted by scenario probabilities.

5. Bayes' Rule

¶ The Inference Problem

Given prior probabilities P(Aᵢ) for different causes and a likelihood model P(B | Aᵢ) describing how likely effect B is under each cause, find the posterior probabilities P(Aᵢ | B) after observing B.

★ Bayes' Rule
$$P(A_i \mid B) = \frac{P(A_i) \cdot P(B \mid A_i)}{\sum_j P(A_j) \cdot P(B \mid A_j)}$$
Numerator is the multiplication rule; denominator is total probability theorem.
✎ Causal vs. Inferential Direction
$$\underbrace{P(B \mid A_i)}_{\text{cause} \to \text{effect}} \quad \xleftrightarrow{\text{Bayes}} \quad \underbrace{P(A_i \mid B)}_{\text{effect} \to \text{cause}}$$
Bayes' rule reverses the direction of conditioning.

6. Case Study: Radar Detection

⊢ Model Setup
$$P(A) = 0.05, \quad P(A^c) = 0.95, \quad P(B \mid A) = 0.99, \quad P(B \mid A^c) = 0.10$$
A = plane present, B = radar detection. Note the low prior for planes and small false alarm rate.
→ Step 1: Total Probability of Detection
$$P(B) = P(A) \cdot P(B \mid A) + P(A^c) \cdot P(B \mid A^c) = (0.05)(0.99) + (0.95)(0.10) = 0.1445$$
True detections (0.0495) plus false alarms (0.095).
→ Step 2: Posterior via Bayes' Rule
$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} = \frac{0.0495}{0.1445} \approx 0.34$$
Despite 99% detection rate, a blip indicates only 34% chance of a plane!
∴ The Base Rate Fallacy
$$\text{False alarms: } 0.95 \times 0.10 = 0.095 \;>\; 0.05 \times 0.99 = 0.0495 \text{ :True detections}$$
The rare base rate (5% planes) means even a 10% false alarm rate dominates.
✎ Medical Testing Analogy
$$P(\text{disease} \mid \text{positive test}) \ll P(\text{positive test} \mid \text{disease}) \text{ when disease is rare}$$
Even accurate tests can mislead for rare conditions—base rates matter enormously.

Key Takeaways

¶ Summary

(1) Countable additivity applies only to sequences, not uncountable unions. (2) Zero probability ≠ impossible in continuous models. (3) Conditional probability = zooming into B as the new universe. (4) Multiplication rule = multiply along tree branches. (5) Total probability = weighted average across scenarios. (6) Bayes' rule = reverse conditional probabilities for inference. (7) Base rates matter: even accurate tests mislead for rare conditions.
