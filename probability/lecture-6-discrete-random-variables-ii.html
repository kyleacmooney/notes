Lecture 6 Discrete Random Variables Ii

This lecture extends the random variable framework with three major concepts: the danger of applying nonlinear functions to expectations, conditional expectations with the Total Expectation Theorem for divide-and-conquer calculations, and the geometric distribution's memorylessness property. It concludes with joint PMFs for analyzing relationships between multiple random variables.

1. Review: Random Variables and Expectation

:= Random Variable
$$X: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$$
A function assigning a real number to each outcome in the sample space.
:= Probability Mass Function
$$p_X(x) = P(X = x)$$
Gives the probability of each numerical outcome.
:= Expectation
$$E[X] = \sum_x x \cdot p_X(x)$$
The weighted average of outcomes, interpretable as the center of gravity of the PMF.
:= LOTUS (Law of the Unconscious Statistician)
$$E[g(X)] = \sum_x g(x) \cdot p_X(x)$$
Compute expected value of g(X) directly from the PMF of X, without finding the PMF of g(X).
★ Linearity of Expectation
$$E[\alpha X + \beta] = \alpha E[X] + \beta$$
The only case where expectation commutes with the function.
✎ Nonlinearity Warning
$$E[g(X)] \neq g(E[X]) \quad \text{in general}$$
A critical principle: you cannot reason 'on the average' with nonlinear functions.

2. Variance and Standard Deviation

¶ Motivation

We want to measure how spread out values are from the mean. A first attempt might be E[X - E[X]], but this always equals zero since positive and negative deviations cancel. The solution is to square the deviations.

:= Variance
$$\text{Var}(X) = E[(X - E[X])^2]$$
Measures the average squared deviation from the mean.
★ Variance Shortcut Formula
$$\text{Var}(X) = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2$$
Often easier to compute than the definition; requires calculating E[X²] and E[X] separately.
:= Standard Deviation
$$\sigma_X = \sqrt{\text{Var}(X)}$$
Returns to the original units of X, making interpretation more natural.

3. Example: Random Speed vs. Random Time

¶ Setup

This example illustrates why expectations don't commute with nonlinear functions. Consider traveling a fixed distance d = 200 miles at a randomly chosen speed V, where P(V = 1) = 1/2 (walking) and P(V = 200) = 1/2 (flying). The travel time is T = 200/V.

= Expected Speed
$$E[V] = \frac{1}{2}(1) + \frac{1}{2}(200) = 100.5 \text{ mph}$$
Straightforward weighted average of the two speeds.
= Expected Time
$$E[T] = \frac{1}{2}(200) + \frac{1}{2}(1) = 100.5 \text{ hours}$$
When V = 1, T = 200 hours; when V = 200, T = 1 hour.
= Product of Expectations
$$E[V] \cdot E[T] = 100.5 \times 100.5 \approx 10{,}100$$
This is NOT equal to E[VT].
= Expectation of Product
$$E[VT] = E[200] = 200$$
Since VT = d = 200 always, this is deterministic.
∴ Key Inequality 1
$$E[VT] \neq E[V] \cdot E[T]$$
The expectation of a product is not the product of expectations (unless variables are independent AND uncorrelated).
∴ Key Inequality 2
$$E\left[\frac{200}{V}\right] = 100.5 \neq \frac{200}{E[V]} = \frac{200}{100.5} \approx 2$$
You cannot compute average time by dividing distance by average speed—this is the nonlinearity trap.

4. Conditional PMF and Conditional Expectation

:= Conditional PMF
$$p_{X \mid A}(x) = P(X = x \mid A)$$
An ordinary PMF in the 'conditional universe' where event A is known to have occurred.
¶ Example: Conditioning on X ≥ 2

Let X be uniform on {1, 2, 3, 4}, so p_X(k) = 1/4 for k = 1, 2, 3, 4. Conditioning on A = {X ≥ 2}: remove probability mass for X = 1 and renormalize. The result is p_{X|A}(k) = 1/3 for k = 2, 3, 4.

:= Conditional Expectation
$$E[X \mid A] = \sum_x x \cdot p_{X \mid A}(x)$$
Ordinary expectation using the conditional PMF.
= Example Calculation
$$E[X \mid X \geq 2] = \frac{1}{3}(2) + \frac{1}{3}(3) + \frac{1}{3}(4) = 3$$
The conditional mean shifts upward since we've excluded the smallest value.
✎ Properties Transfer
$$\text{Linearity, LOTUS, etc. all hold for conditional expectations}$$
Conditional expectations ARE ordinary expectations, just in a different probability space.

5. Total Expectation Theorem

¶ Motivation

The Total Expectation Theorem provides a divide-and-conquer method for computing expectations. Rather than computing E[X] directly, we partition into scenarios, compute the conditional expectation in each, and combine with a weighted average.

★ Total Expectation Theorem
$$E[X] = \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i) \cdot E[X \mid A_i]$$
Requires A₁, A₂, ..., Aₙ to form a partition of the sample space (mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive).
✎ Intuition
$$\text{Overall average} = \text{weighted average of conditional averages}$$
Weights are the scenario probabilities P(Aᵢ).
✎ Connection to Total Probability
$$\begin{aligned} \text{Total Probability:} \quad & P(B) = \sum_i P(A_i)P(B \mid A_i) \\ \text{Total Expectation:} \quad & E[X] = \sum_i P(A_i)E[X \mid A_i] \end{aligned}$$
Same divide-and-conquer structure, but for expectations instead of event probabilities.

6. Geometric Random Variable

:= Geometric Random Variable
$$X = \text{number of independent tosses until first Head, where } P(\text{Head}) = p$$
Models waiting time for the first success in repeated Bernoulli trials.
:= Geometric PMF
$$p_X(k) = (1-p)^{k-1} p \quad \text{for } k = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$$
Requires k-1 tails followed by 1 head; the (1-p)^{k-1} factor decays geometrically.

6.1. Expected Value via Total Expectation

¶ Strategy

We partition by the outcome of the first toss. This creates a recursive structure that we can solve algebraically.

→ Case 1: First Toss is Heads
$$P(\text{1st is H}) = p, \quad E[X \mid \text{1st is H}] = 1$$
If the first toss is heads, we're done immediately.
→ Case 2: First Toss is Tails
$$P(\text{1st is T}) = 1-p, \quad E[X \mid \text{1st is T}] = 1 + E[X]$$
We've 'wasted' one toss and start fresh—the remaining tosses have the same distribution as the original X.
→ Apply Total Expectation
$$E[X] = p \cdot 1 + (1-p)(1 + E[X])$$
Weighted average of the two cases.
→ Expand
$$E[X] = p + (1-p) + (1-p)E[X] = 1 + (1-p)E[X]$$
Note that p + (1-p) = 1.
→ Solve for E[X]
$$E[X] - (1-p)E[X] = 1 \implies pE[X] = 1$$
Collect E[X] terms on the left side.
∴ Expected Value of Geometric
$$\boxed{E[X] = \frac{1}{p}}$$
Intuition: if p is small (heads is rare), 1/p is large—you expect to wait a long time.

6.2. Memorylessness Property

¶ Key Insight

Independent coin flips have no memory. The fact that you flipped tails previously doesn't change the probability of future flips. This leads to a remarkable property unique to the geometric distribution.

★ Memorylessness
$$P(X - n = k \mid X > n) = P(X = k) \quad \text{for all } n, k \geq 1$$
Given that you've already waited n tosses (all tails), the remaining wait time has the same distribution as starting fresh.
→ Geometric Decay Property
$$\frac{p_X(k+1)}{p_X(k)} = \frac{(1-p)^k p}{(1-p)^{k-1} p} = (1-p) \quad \text{(constant)}$$
The ratio between consecutive PMF values is constant—this is why conditioning just shifts the PMF.
¶ Visual Argument

Plot the geometric PMF as bars at k = 1, 2, 3, ... with geometrically decaying heights. Conditioning on X > n removes the first n bars. The remaining bars maintain their relative proportions (constant ratio), so after renormalizing they form an identical copy of the original PMF, just shifted. This constant-ratio property is unique to the geometric distribution among discrete distributions.

7. Joint PMFs

¶ Motivation

To study relationships between random variables (e.g., height vs. weight, or correlated measurements), individual PMFs are insufficient. We need to capture joint probabilities—the probability of specific combinations of values occurring together.

:= Joint PMF
$$p_{X,Y}(x, y) = P(X = x \text{ and } Y = y)$$
Captures the probability of each combination of values for X and Y.
⊢ Properties of Joint PMF
$$\begin{aligned} &(1) \quad p_{X,Y}(x, y) \geq 0 \\ &(2) \quad \sum_x \sum_y p_{X,Y}(x, y) = 1 \end{aligned}$$
Same non-negativity and normalization as single-variable PMFs.

7.1. Marginal PMFs

:= Marginal PMF
$$p_X(x) = \sum_y p_{X,Y}(x, y)$$
Recover the individual PMF of X by summing out Y over all its possible values.
✎ Table Interpretation
$$\text{Sum across a row (or column) to get marginal probability}$$
In a joint probability table, marginals appear as row/column totals.

7.2. Conditional PMF from Joint

:= Conditional PMF from Joint
$$p_{X \mid Y}(x \mid y) = \frac{p_{X,Y}(x, y)}{p_Y(y)}$$
Follows directly from the definition of conditional probability P(A|B) = P(A∩B)/P(B).
¶ Interpretation

To find the conditional PMF of X given Y = y: (1) Fix a value of Y, selecting one row or column of the joint table. (2) The entries in that slice give relative probabilities. (3) Divide by p_Y(y) to renormalize so probabilities sum to 1. The conditional PMF is just a slice of the joint PMF, rescaled.

Key Takeaways

∴ Takeaway 1
$$E[g(X)] \neq g(E[X]) \text{ in general}$$
Don't reason 'on the average' with products, ratios, or other nonlinear functions.
∴ Takeaway 2
$$\text{Conditional expectations are ordinary expectations in a conditional universe}$$
All standard properties (linearity, LOTUS) transfer directly.
∴ Takeaway 3
$$E[X] = \sum_i P(A_i) E[X \mid A_i]$$
Total Expectation Theorem: divide and conquer by partitioning into scenarios.
∴ Takeaway 4
$$X \sim \text{Geometric}(p) \implies E[X] = \frac{1}{p}$$
Derived elegantly using total expectation and the memoryless property.
∴ Takeaway 5
$$\text{Memorylessness: } P(X - n = k \mid X > n) = P(X = k)$$
Given past failures, remaining wait time equals starting fresh—unique to geometric among discrete distributions.
∴ Takeaway 6
$$p_X(x) = \sum_y p_{X,Y}(x,y), \quad p_{X|Y}(x|y) = \frac{p_{X,Y}(x,y)}{p_Y(y)}$$
Joint PMFs capture relationships; marginals are sums; conditionals are renormalized slices.
