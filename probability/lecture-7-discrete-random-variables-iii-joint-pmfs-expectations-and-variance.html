Lecture 7 Discrete Random Variables Iii Joint Pmfs Expectations And Variance

Extending probability concepts from pairs to multiple random variables, establishing when expectations and variances of sums simplify (linearity always works for expectations, but variance addition requires independence), and demonstrating the indicator variable technique through the binomial distribution and the classic hat problem.

¶ Lecture Overview

This lecture concludes Chapter 2 on discrete random variables, extending concepts from pairs to multiple random variables. The central themes are linearity of expectations (which holds unconditionally), variance of sums (which requires independence), and the power of indicator variable decomposition.

1. Joint and Conditional PMFs

:= Joint PMF
$$p_{X,Y}(x,y) = P(X=x, Y=y)$$
Probability that the pair of values (x,y) occurs simultaneously.
:= Conditional PMF
$$p_{X \mid Y}(x \mid y) = P(X=x \mid Y=y)$$
The probability law on X when we condition on observing Y=y.
:= Marginal PMF
$$p_X(x) = P(X=x)$$
Probability that X takes value x, ignoring Y entirely.
★ Marginalization
$$p_X(x) = \sum_y p_{X,Y}(x,y)$$
To recover the marginal, sum the joint over all values of the other variable.
★ Multiplication Rule for PMFs
$$p_{X,Y}(x,y) = p_X(x) \cdot p_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x)$$
Analogous to P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B|A) for events.

2. Extension to Multiple Random Variables

:= Joint PMF for Three Variables
$$p_{X,Y,Z}(x,y,z) = P(X=x, Y=y, Z=z)$$
Natural extension: probability of a triple of values.
★ Marginalization for Three Variables
$$p_X(x) = \sum_y \sum_z p_{X,Y,Z}(x,y,z)$$
Fix x and sum over all combinations of the other variables.
★ Chain Rule for Three Variables
$$p_{X,Y,Z}(x,y,z) = p_X(x) \cdot p_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) \cdot p_{Z \mid X,Y}(z \mid x,y)$$
Sequential conditioning: probability of first, times second given first, times third given first two.

3. Independence of Random Variables

:= Independence of Random Variables
$$X, Y, Z \text{ are independent} \iff p_{X,Y,Z}(x,y,z) = p_X(x) \cdot p_Y(y) \cdot p_Z(z) \quad \forall x,y,z$$
Joint PMF factors into product of marginals for ALL possible values.
◇ Equivalent Characterization
$$X \perp Y \iff p_{X \mid Y}(x \mid y) = p_X(x) \quad \forall x,y$$
Independence means conditioning on Y provides no information about X.
✎ Conditional Independence
$$X \perp Y \mid A \text{ means independence holds within the restricted universe where } A \text{ occurred}$$
Variables may be dependent overall but independent when conditioning on some event A.

4. Expectations

★ LOTUS for Joint Distributions
$$E[g(X,Y)] = \sum_x \sum_y g(x,y) \cdot p_{X,Y}(x,y)$$
Law of the Unconscious Statistician extends to functions of multiple variables.

4.1. Linearity of Expectations

★ Linearity of Expectation (Scaling and Shifting)
$$E[\alpha X + \beta] = \alpha E[X] + \beta$$
Constants factor out and can be added/subtracted.
★ Linearity of Expectation (Sums)
$$E[X + Y + Z] = E[X] + E[Y] + E[Z]$$
ALWAYS TRUE regardless of independence—this is the most important property.
✎ SAT Score Analogy
$$\text{Average total SAT} = \sum (\text{average of each section})$$
You can average sections separately then add, or average totals directly—same result.

4.2. Expectations of Products

★ Product Rule (Requires Independence)
$$X \perp Y \implies E[XY] = E[X] \cdot E[Y]$$
Only valid for independent variables; generally E[XY] ≠ E[X]E[Y].
◇ Functions of Independent Variables
$$X \perp Y \implies E[g(X)h(Y)] = E[g(X)] \cdot E[h(Y)]$$
If X is independent of Y, then g(X) is independent of h(Y) for any functions g, h.

5. Variances

★ Variance Scaling
$$\text{var}(\alpha X) = \alpha^2 \text{var}(X)$$
Constants come out squared (variance measures squared deviations).
★ Variance Shifting
$$\text{var}(X + \beta) = \text{var}(X)$$
Adding a constant shifts the distribution but doesn't change its spread.
★ Variance of Sum (Requires Independence)
$$X \perp Y \implies \text{var}(X+Y) = \text{var}(X) + \text{var}(Y)$$
Unlike expectation, variance addition requires independence.
◇ Variance of Difference
$$X \perp Y \implies \text{var}(X-Y) = \text{var}(X) + (-1)^2\text{var}(Y) = \text{var}(X) + \text{var}(Y)$$
Variances still ADD for differences (minus sign squares away).
✎ Counterexamples for Dependent Variables
$$\begin{aligned} Y = X &\implies \text{var}(X+Y) = \text{var}(2X) = 4\text{var}(X) \neq 2\text{var}(X) \\ Y = -X &\implies \text{var}(X+Y) = \text{var}(0) = 0 \neq 2\text{var}(X) \end{aligned}$$
Perfect positive or negative correlation breaks the additive formula.

6. Application: Binomial Mean and Variance

¶ Setup

Consider n independent coin flips with P(heads) = p. Let X = total number of heads. Rather than working with binomial coefficients directly, we decompose X using indicator variables.

:= Indicator Variables
$$X_i = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if trial } i \text{ is success} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$
Binary variables that 'indicate' whether an event occurred.
= Decomposition
$$X = X_1 + X_2 + \cdots + X_n$$
The total count equals the sum of indicators—key insight for this technique.

6.1. Mean of Binomial

→ Expectation of Each Indicator
$$E[X_i] = 1 \cdot p + 0 \cdot (1-p) = p$$
Expectation of a Bernoulli(p) random variable.
→ Apply Linearity
$$E[X] = \sum_{i=1}^n E[X_i] = np$$
No messy algebra with binomial coefficients needed.
∴ Binomial Mean
$$X \sim \text{Binomial}(n,p) \implies E[X] = np$$
The expected number of successes equals trials times probability.

6.2. Variance of Binomial

→ Key Property of Indicators
$$X_i^2 = X_i \quad (\text{since } 0^2=0, 1^2=1)$$
Squaring an indicator returns the same indicator.
→ Variance of Each Indicator
$$\text{var}(X_i) = E[X_i^2] - (E[X_i])^2 = p - p^2 = p(1-p)$$
Using the formula var(X) = E[X²] - (E[X])².
→ Variances Add (Independence)
$$\text{var}(X) = \sum_{i=1}^n \text{var}(X_i) = np(1-p)$$
Since trials are independent, indicator variances add.
∴ Binomial Variance
$$X \sim \text{Binomial}(n,p) \implies \text{var}(X) = np(1-p)$$
Variance is maximized at p=1/2 (fair coin = most uncertainty).

7. Application: The Hat Problem

¶ Problem Statement

n people throw their hats into a box and each picks one at random (all permutations equally likely). Let X = number of people who get their own hat back. Find E[X] and var(X).

:= Indicator Setup
$$X_i = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if person } i \text{ gets own hat} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \quad \Rightarrow \quad X = \sum_{i=1}^n X_i$$
Same decomposition technique as binomial, but indicators are NOT independent here.

7.1. Expected Value

→ Probability Each Gets Own Hat
$$P(X_i = 1) = \frac{1}{n}$$
By symmetry, each of the n hats is equally likely to be picked by person i.
→ Apply Linearity (Despite Dependence!)
$$E[X] = \sum_{i=1}^n E[X_i] = n \cdot \frac{1}{n} = 1$$
Linearity holds even though knowing 9 of 10 people got their hats determines the 10th.
∴ Hat Problem Mean
$$E[X] = 1 \quad \text{for all } n$$
On average, exactly 1 person gets their own hat back, regardless of group size.

7.2. Variance (Handling Dependence)

¶ Strategy

Since the X_i are dependent, we cannot simply add variances. Instead, we compute E[X²] directly using the expansion of (∑X_i)².

→ Expand X²
$$X^2 = \left(\sum_i X_i\right)^2 = \sum_i X_i^2 + \sum_{i \neq j} X_i X_j$$
Squared sum splits into diagonal terms (i=j) and cross terms (i≠j).
→ Diagonal Terms
$$E\left[\sum_i X_i^2\right] = E\left[\sum_i X_i\right] = E[X] = 1$$
Using X_i² = X_i for indicators.
→ Cross Terms: Setup
$$E[X_i X_j] = P(X_i=1, X_j=1) = P(X_i=1) \cdot P(X_j=1 \mid X_i=1)$$
Product X_i X_j = 1 only when both get their own hats.
→ Cross Terms: Calculation
$$P(X_j=1 \mid X_i=1) = \frac{1}{n-1} \implies E[X_i X_j] = \frac{1}{n} \cdot \frac{1}{n-1}$$
Given person i got their hat, person j picks from n-1 remaining hats.
→ Sum of Cross Terms
$$\sum_{i \neq j} E[X_i X_j] = n(n-1) \cdot \frac{1}{n(n-1)} = 1$$
There are n(n-1) ordered pairs (i,j) with i≠j.
→ Compute E[X²]
$$E[X^2] = 1 + 1 = 2$$
Diagonal terms contribute 1, cross terms contribute 1.
∴ Hat Problem Variance
$$\text{var}(X) = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2 = 2 - 1 = 1$$
Both mean and variance equal 1, independent of n—a remarkable result.

Key Takeaways

✎ Linearity of Expectation
$$E\left[\sum_i X_i\right] = \sum_i E[X_i] \quad \text{ALWAYS}$$
No independence required—this makes indicator variables powerful even for dependent problems.
✎ Variance of Sums
$$\text{var}\left(\sum_i X_i\right) = \sum_i \text{var}(X_i) \quad \text{ONLY IF INDEPENDENT}$$
For dependent variables, must use var(X) = E[X²] - (E[X])² directly.
✎ Indicator Variable Technique
$$\text{Count} = \sum (\text{indicators}) \implies E[\text{Count}] = \sum P(\text{each event})$$
Decompose complex counts into sums of 0/1 variables for easier expectation calculations.
